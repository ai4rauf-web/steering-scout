i. What Scout actually is

The product, as it stands today.

Scout is a full-bleed chat surface. One text field, one send button, and a scrolling stream of Scout's replies. There are no filters, no facets, no map, no card grid — the entire product runs on prose.

The empty state offers a small quick-search rail (New launches this month, Under 20% down payment, Emaar & DAMAC launches) and a "Market Pulse" strip of live figures. The composer greets you in a rotating language and promises to "search in the language you think in."

When you speak, Scout replies in tight paragraphs with an inline list of properties — tower, size, price, status — followed by market context it decides is relevant. A small Used 2 tools · 2.7s tag hints that a search and an analysis ran under the hood, but their inputs stay hidden.

Nothing on the screen tells you which of your words Scout took literally, which it interpreted, and which it invented. That gap is the entire brief.

ii. A real walkthrough

What happens when I actually use it.

I opened Scout on a 375-pixel viewport and typed the kind of thing a real buyer would say — one sentence, four constraints packed together. Scout came back in under three seconds with a competent answer.

The answer is well-written. But look at what's not in it: no acknowledgement that I said "ready to move in" and Scout heard it as "furnished". No note that 2M AED got quietly stretched to include a 2.1M listing. No visible link between my sentence and the query that ran.

If Scout is right, I'm delighted. If Scout is subtly wrong, I have to spot the drift from the results — and results are the most expensive place to catch a bug.

iii. The audit that grounds the brief

What today's entry screen tells you Scout isn't.

Before designing steering, I audited the surface that produces the problem. Two views — the mobile hero at 375 pixels, and the desktop hero at ~1440 — reveal the same defect at both scales: the entry screen is designed as a marketing landing page for a search product, but Scout is a conversational LLM. Everything else in this document sits on top of that gap.

The strategic finding

The empty state is a landing page. Scout is a chat.

Above the fold: a photo, a marketing paragraph, six keyword pills. The interaction model is invisible. A first-time user has no signal that Scout understands natural language, no example of what to ask, and no visible way back into last night's conversation.

How this feeds the steering brief. The audit surfaces two classes of debt. Micro defects — contrast, target size, truncation — are the ordinary maintenance a design team catches in review. The strategic finding is different: it's the reason the steering problem exists at all. Scout captures preferences silently in tool inputs because the surface never asked the user to think of the interaction as a conversation. Fix the empty state, and the steering problem becomes softer. Design steering without fixing the empty state, and every solution has to compensate for a wrong first impression.

iv. The actual problem

Three questions, hiding as one.

The brief reads as a single problem — how does the user steer? — but it's really three questions stacked on top of each other, and any solution has to answer all three without borrowing the filter panel it was hired to avoid.

The trap is obvious once you look at it: pin a chip strip to the top of the chat, or spring a bottom sheet titled Understood preferences when the user taps a settings glyph. Both are filter panels wearing a chat costume. They fail the brief on its own terms.

Visibility How does the user see the model Scout is operating on — without breaking the conversational flow?
Correction How can they disagree with any single piece of it in one thumb tap, without a filter panel?
Steering How can they add a priority, drop an old one, or branch into a new angle mid-conversation?

v. Where the friction lives

Three moments a real user meets.

Correction isn't a screen — it's a moment. Design the moments, and the surface follows. Every friction I mapped landed on one of three:

Moment A After Scout replies.
User's inner voice
"Did Scout actually understand what I said, or did it fill in blanks?"
What Scout knows
Structured filters + a confidence score for each one, held in memory.
The opportunity
A glanceable summary inside the message the user just read — no new surface.
Failure mode
Any chip strip. It's a filter panel.
Moment B While composing a reply.
User's inner voice
"I want to change one thing, but I don't want to re-explain everything."
What Scout knows
The most-likely corrections given the conversation so far.
The opportunity
Predictive chips inside the composer that stand in for typing — one tap replaces a sentence.
Failure mode
Ghosting the composer with too many suggestions, becoming intrusive.
Moment C After a misunderstanding.
User's inner voice
"These aren't what I meant. Roll back."
What Scout knows
Which inferences produced this reply, and which prior message introduced each one.
The opportunity
Undo scoped to a specific claim — unsay a phrase, not the whole turn.
Failure mode
Making correction more expensive than retyping. Users just leave.

vi. What conversation uniquely gives us

Six advantages a filter panel cannot claim.

The temptation, once the brief's constraints sink in, is to design filters more cleverly. The stronger move is to ask what a language surface can do that no facet grid ever can — and let that answer set the design bar.

AdvantageWhat it meansDesign implication
Provenance Every preference carries a "you said this, in these words, at this time." Tap any preference to see the sentence that created it — quoted verbatim.
Confidence Scout knows what was stated vs. what it guessed. Render inferences differently from statements — visual weight, not badges.
Time Preferences are a stack of turns, not a set of toggles. Unsay a phrase; rewind to the state before you said Marina.
Compound "2M, or 2.3M if it's beach-facing" — a conditional a filter can't hold. Soft-rule pills that carry a tunable trade, not a binary.
Negation "Avoid ground floor" is a first-class thought, not an inverse filter. "Avoid" reads and edits as its own preference type.
Interpretation "Family-friendly" expands to schools, parks, community rating. Show the expansion inline; let the user prune the parts.

vii. Principles I designed against

Four bars that killed the easy ideas.

I wrote these before drawing anything, and used them to reject my first three concepts. They're the reason the final direction isn't a chip strip.

Provenance over panel.
If it could exist as a filter panel with the labels changed, it's not conversational. Every preference must point back to the words that made it.
Correction cheaper than typing.
If a fix takes more taps or keystrokes than re-explaining, users just re-explain — and that's how filter-panel patterns win back the surface.
Confidence is a visual property.
Stated and inferred are different objects with different rights. They cannot look the same, or the user can't tell what to trust.
Undo before UI.
A gesture to unsay something belongs in the interaction model before any new surface does. Reversibility is the cheapest trust primitive we have.

viii. Divergent concepts

Eight sketches, three I'd advance.

Each concept is scored against the three friction moments (A / B / C) and the four principles above. Three cleared both bars — they carry the brick line across the top.

01 In-message provenance highlights. Every phrase in Scout's reply that came from a preference is subtly underlined — amber for stated, dusk-dashed for inferred. Tap the phrase to open the sentence that created it, edit or unsay it in place. Moments A · C
02 Composer prediction pills. The moment the user taps the composer, three predictive pills appear above the keyboard: drop "furnished," widen budget to 2.2M, add "sea view." One tap replaces a sentence's worth of typing. Moment B
03 Long-press to unsay. Long-pressing any provenance-highlighted phrase in a Scout reply reveals a single "Unsay this" action. It removes the source sentence's inference and re-runs the last tool call. No modal, no confirm. Moment C
04 "Currently looking for..." sentence. Scout keeps writing a one-sentence self-summary above its latest reply. Words in the sentence are tappable. Reads like language, not filters. Softer sibling of 01; risk of duplication
05 Pull-down memory card. Pull the top of the chat down to reveal a card of "what Scout remembers." Editable inline. Closest to a panel — kept in the pile as a benchmark. Fallback if 01 fails testing
06 Swipe-left to rewind a turn. Swipe left on a Scout message to unsend the corresponding user turn. Interaction is intuitive; the mental model of removing a whole turn is heavier than a phrase. Too coarse; consolidated into 03
07 "What do you know about me?" A meta-utterance the user can type or tap. Scout replies with a structured self-summary. Fun, but requires the user to know the trick. Discoverability risk; kept as ambient hint
08 Ambient "based on" line. Small caption under each reply: "Based on 3 things you said, 2 I guessed." Tapping reveals the guesses. Elegant, but shifts work to a second screen. Layer under 01, not a standalone

ix. The chosen direction

Provenance, in the message itself.

Concepts 01, 02 and 03 combine into one interaction model. Provenance lives inside Scout's replies as typographic weight; correction happens inside the composer, before the user commits to typing; unsay lives on long-press, one gesture below every claim.

Below is the model walking through one loop — read, correct, resume — on 375 × 812.

Frame 01 · Read Scout replies. Provenance is in the text. Amber underline = user's own words. Dashed dusk underline = Scout's inference. The user learns the vocabulary in one glance; the annotation isn't in the final UI.
Frame 02 · Correct Long-press an inferred phrase. Unsay it. The card is anchored to the word, not a global sheet. Provenance shows the exact source phrase. A live preview shows the delta before commit — no confirm modal needed.
Frame 03 · Steer Composer predicts the next correction. The moment the composer opens, three predictions appear — derived from the current inferred model and typical next moves. One tap replaces the sentence the user was about to write.

What the RTL trio proves. The steering system was designed for language, not for a specific axis. The provenance vocabulary, the long-press unsay card, the composer prediction pattern — every one of them mirrors without introducing a new interaction. That's what "designed for RTL from day one" actually looks like in a mid-conversation flow, and it's why Section xi could confidently claim direction is an emergent property of language: because nothing here fights the flip.

x. Tradeoffs & what I'd test next

Honest costs of the chosen direction.

Every one of these deserves a research question, not a defense. Listed with the risk I'd want to disprove first.

ConcernWhat it costsHow I'd test
Discoverability Underlines are subtle. A first-time user may not know the phrases are tappable. First-run coach mark on the first inferred word only; measure first-tap rate over 3 sessions.
Legibility Two underline styles inside prose could feel busy on longer replies. Cap provenance highlights to N=3 per reply; overflow renders as plain text with a "… + 2 more" affordance.
Latency Unsay re-runs a tool call. On slow networks, the user waits without a result. Optimistic swap: the reply's affected phrases dim in place; only listings re-render on tool return.
Prediction quality Composer pills that miss make the composer feel noisy. Show pills only when model confidence > threshold; log tap-through vs. dismiss.
Arabic ambiguity Code-switched sentences ("ابحث عن 2BR في Marina") complicate provenance quoting. Store provenance at the token level with language tag; render the quote in the original script.
Trust drift over long sessions After 20 turns, provenance chains get long — Scout's model of the user is complex. "What do you know about me?" utterance flattens to a scannable summary on demand.

xi. Language & direction

When Arabic arrives, the whole surface flips.

Property Finder promises "search in the language you think in." That claim only holds if the interface moves with the language — not just the input. RTL isn't a preference to bury in settings; it's an interaction pattern that has to kick in the moment a user's first sentence is Arabic. And it has to stay per-conversation — a person might chat in Arabic with a broker in one thread and English about mortgage rates in another.

The principle

The language is the direction.

Auto-detect from what the user actually writes. Flip silently on the first message, offer politely on any switch after. Never ask a first-time user to find a settings toggle.

Five moments the design has to answer.

  1. 01
    First message is Arabic — flip silently.
    The moment the opening message reads as high-confidence Arabic (script ratio ≥ 0.5, ≥ 4 words), the surface transitions to RTL in one 250 ms animation. The composer chip flips EN → AR. No modal, no confirmation. Trust the detection.
  2. 02
    Mid-conversation switch — direction belongs to the turn.
    My first answer here was “keep the layout, offer a switch.” It doesn't survive a thread that goes English → 中文 → العربية. The better rule: each question-and-answer renders in its own language and direction. The Arabic turn is RTL; the English and Chinese turns above it stay exactly as written. Nothing already on screen ever moves, so there is nothing to be disoriented by — and nothing to ask permission for.
  3. 03
    Manual override lives in the composer chip.
    The EN / AR chip in the composer is the single source of truth. Tapping it opens a compact sheet with every supported language. Selecting one locks the direction for this thread and auto-detection stops. No other settings surface carries a language toggle.
  4. 04
    Code-switched messages — don't guess, hold.
    "ابحث عن apartment في Marina under 2M" is 30% Arabic, 70% Latin. Confidence is low, and MENA users routinely code-switch. Keep the current direction. Add a small dot on the composer chip to signal detection saw mixed content, and let the next full-sentence message decide.
  5. 05
    Resumed conversations remember.
    Every thread stores its direction. A conversation opened in Arabic three days ago reopens in RTL — no re-detection, no flicker. If the user changes language later, moment 02 applies. Direction is a property of the thread, not the app.

Three languages, one thread.

A Dubai buyer might open in English, add a detail in Chinese, then ask the next question in Arabic. Testing the live product showed what goes wrong today: the reply comes back half-translated, with English labels inside Arabic sentences. Six rules came out of building this case into the prototype.

RuleWhat it means on screen
Direction belongs to the turnEach turn carries its own lang and dir. English → 中文 changes no layout at all; only a move into Arabic mirrors, and only for that turn.
Scout follows you, silentlyReply, labels, thinking lines, Refine rows and the provenance card localise per turn. So do prices (2.7 مليون درهم) and grammar: Arabic nouns agree with their count — ٤ شقق but ٢٣ شقة — so the noun is chosen by the number beside it, never fixed.
Names: localised first, Latin keptA listing shows its name in the reply language (مارينا جيت 1) with the Latin name small beneath it (Marina Gate 1). A reader needs a name they can read — but the Latin one is what is on the building, the contract and the map, and what a broker will say, so it is never replaced. One listing ID, one canonical name, one localised name per language; where no official localised name exists, only the Latin shows.
Navigation stays putI first mirrored the header too, and reversed it on sight: Back jumping sides mid-thread breaks muscle memory, and the phone's language hasn't changed. Only where you read and write follows the language — the turns and the composer.
One tap back, at the point of changeWhere the language switches, a marker says so and offers Reply in English instead — the language the thread began in. It holds until undone. This covers misdetection, and people who write Arabic but want English answers.
What you said carries across languages“Under 2M”, said in English, is still stated in the Arabic answer. The provenance card quotes your words in the script you wrote them in. In the demo thread this is where it pays off: asked in Arabic for three bedrooms, Scout quietly raised the budget to 2.8M — and the card surfaces it, quoting the English “under 2M” it overrode.

One craft detail worth naming: letter-spacing is removed inside Arabic. It is a joined script, and tracking pulls the letters apart — the uppercase-and-tracked label style that works in Latin quietly breaks Arabic words.

xii. Living inside PF's identity

The proposal, translated into PF's design system.

I extracted PF's design system directly from propertyfinder.ae/scout/v2 — Open Sans across the whole product, the secondary-07 purple #3A307F as the interactive accent, and shadows tinted with that same purple at graduated alphas. The proposal maps onto it cleanly. Even the provenance vocabulary — the one thing that could have felt foreign — translates: stated becomes a solid secondary-07 underline, inferred becomes a dashed secondary-05 #8C84CC stroke. Same interaction, same intent, PF's palette.

What this proves. The proposal doesn't ask PF to adopt a new visual language to ship. Every design decision maps to a token that already exists — or, in the two places PF's system is thin (no display serif, no monospace face), Open Sans covers cleanly using weight and tracking. If Scout's team wanted to try one concept in production overnight, it can wear the existing UI straight through.

Related: the audit's biggest fix — the hero contrast defect (finding A1) — is a one-line change. The token --gradient-hero-overlay already exists in :root and is currently set to none. Setting it to linear-gradient(180deg, transparent, rgba(0,0,0,0.55)) resolves the failure without a single new token.

xiii. The prototype

It runs. Open it on your phone.

Everything above is built as a working prototype: seven states at a true 375 pixels, in Property Finder's own tokens, in light and dark. On a laptop it sits in a phone frame with a rail to jump between states; on a phone the frame drops away and it behaves like an app.

Why voice leads. The audit's clearest behavioural note was that people arrive wanting to talk, and the product offers “type and send.” Speaking runs at roughly 150 words a minute against 40 for typing, so a detailed brief takes under 90 seconds instead of five minutes, with fewer errors. Voice therefore sits in the composer with the same weight as send, and takes the whole screen when used: a sphere that visibly listens, a transcript you can watch forming, and a confirm step so nothing is sent that you didn't say.

  • Hold a dashed phrase in any answer. The card quotes the words Scout inferred from; Unsay drops the assumption and re-answers.
  • Tap the waveform for voice: listening, then confirm and send.
  • Tap “Three languages” in the rail: one thread in English, Chinese and Arabic, each turn in its own direction.
  • Tap your avatar for conversation history; pick one to resume it.

What is simulated. Answers are scripted rather than generated, because provenance needs structure — each phrase records whether you said it or Scout assumed it. Voice transcription is simulated so the demo is repeatable. Listing photos are illustrative renders, not the named buildings.
